Reasoning at Every Scale: Molecules, Cells, and Patients

Vision models that link evidence from molecules, cells, tissues and organs, and patients

Clinical and biological questions are answered with evidence at different scales, from gene expression and cell mechanics to histology slides, radiology scans, and patient records. RES-Med will focus on vision-centered models that reason across these scales, and on whether that reasoning remains accurate, computationally tractable, and reliable.

Proposed for: CVPR 2027 workshops, June 20–21, 2027 Venue: Seattle, USA (with the main conference) Format: Half day, in person, talks recorded
About the workshop Topics Organizers

Why this workshop

A tumor, for example, is described by its gene expression, by the shape and stiffness of its cells, by its appearance in histology slides and radiology scans, and by the patient's symptoms, wearable signals, and outcomes. Most vision models for medicine are trained and evaluated at one of these scales. In our analysis of recent CVPR, WACV, ICCV, and ECCV papers, only 18% of biomedical vision abstracts mention computational efficiency, and 21% mention generalization, calibration, or fairness.

RES-Med will bring together researchers who work at four scales: molecules (imaging–omics), cells (microscopy and nanomechanics), tissues and organs (efficient medical image analysis), and patients (digital health). We will ask every contribution to report the scales it links, how its data are paired across them, its accuracy, its computational requirements, and at least one reliability check.

Why a vision workshop? Every contribution will use images, video, volumes, or slides as a primary input or output; evidence from other scales adds context to a visual task rather than replacing it.

Questions we want to answer

  1. When one scale is missing at the point of care, how much accuracy does a cross-scale model lose, and how should it fail?
  2. Do image-derived representations contain molecular or cellular signal, and how should that signal be tested?
  3. In a clinic with modest hardware, what gain in accuracy justifies a tenfold increase in computation or memory?
  4. Which reliability checks (for example, external sites, calibration, subgroups) should be standard for models that link scales?

Four scales

Molecules

Imaging–omics and biological representation

  • Histology with spatial or bulk transcriptomics
  • Radiogenomics and molecular subtyping
  • Image-like representations of omics data
Cells

Cell microscopy and nanomechanics

  • Atomic force microscopy (AFM) of live cells and biomolecules
  • Nanomechanical maps linked to molecular state
  • Automated microscope control and analysis
Tissues and organs

Efficient medical image analysis

  • 2D and 3D segmentation, detection, registration
  • Lightweight decoders, pruning, quantization
  • Edge and on-device deployment
Patients

Digital health and clinical translation

  • Images with health records and clinical text
  • Wearable and physiological signals
  • Outcomes, workflow, and external validation

Across all scales: limited labels and paired data · missing scales · cross-institution generalization and calibration · interpretability grounded in biology or anatomy · computation, memory, and latency · fairness, privacy, and clinically meaningful evaluation.

Topics of interest

  • Molecules: imaging–omics integration, including histology with spatial or bulk transcriptomics, radiogenomics, and image-like representations of omics data
  • Cells: cell and molecular microscopy, including AFM imaging, nanomechanical maps, and automated microscope control
  • Tissues and organs: efficient 2D and 3D segmentation, detection, and registration, including compression and edge deployment
  • Patients: images with health records, clinical text, wearable and physiological signals, and longitudinal follow-up
  • Models, including vision–language models, that link evidence across two or more scales and state which evidence supports each prediction
  • Learning when one scale is missing, or when paired samples across scales are scarce
  • Calibration, uncertainty, cross-institution generalization, and subgroup fairness of cross-scale models
  • Datasets with data paired across scales, and benchmarks that measure accuracy, computational requirements, and reliability together

Does my paper fit?

Strong submissions put visual data at the center of the task and report the four items of the cross-scale reporting standard: (1) the scales the model links and how the data are paired across them, (2) accuracy, (3) a resource profile (for example, parameters, floating-point operations (FLOPs) or latency, memory, and hardware), and (4) at least one reliability check, such as external-site testing, behavior when a scale is missing, calibration, or subgroup results.

Work on non-image data alone (for example, omics or electronic health record (EHR) models without a visual task) is better suited to other venues.

Call for papers

This call is provisional, subject to workshop acceptance and final CVPR rules. Submissions are not open.

Scope

We invite vision-centered work that links medical or biological images, video, volumes, or slides to evidence at another scale: molecular data, cell measurements, clinical records or text, or wearable signals. We will ask every paper to report four items together: the scales it links and how its data are paired, accuracy, computational requirements, and at least one reliability check.

Planned tracks

The workshop will be fully non-archival: no paper will appear in the CVPR or Computer Vision Foundation (CVF) proceedings. Four tracks are planned: full papers (up to 8 pages), tiny papers (up to 2 pages), negative results (up to 4 pages), and grand-challenge papers (up to 4 pages) that each state one open problem in reasoning across scales; the grand-challenge track is a paper track, not a competition. Review policy and deadlines will be announced if the workshop is accepted.

Community and ethics

  • Responsible data use: state data provenance, licenses, and approvals; use patient-level splits to prevent leakage.
  • Fairness and shift: report external-site and subgroup results where possible.
  • Clinical validity: explanations should be tested against known biology or anatomy; research models are not clinical devices.
  • Students: student-led papers and volunteers are especially welcome.
